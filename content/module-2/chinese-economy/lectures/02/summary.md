# Lecture 2: China's Institutions

> **In one sentence:** China created strong growth incentives without Western-style market institutions by combining **political centralization** with **economic decentralization**, so local officials competed and experimented for growth, but the same system later produced land dependence, pollution and distorted incentives.

## 1. The institutional puzzle

- Average GDP growth: **4.4% before 1978**, **9.5% after 1978**. 800+ million people out of extreme poverty.
- **Standard view:** property rights → market institutions → growth.
- **China:** partial reforms → rapid growth → **?** Early on it had weak private property protection, incomplete contract enforcement and limited financial markets. Yet firms and local governments had strong incentives to expand.

**Main question:** *how did China create growth incentives without fully adopting Western-style market institutions?*

**Key idea of the lecture:** institutions shape incentives. China created them through **decentralization + competition + experimentation**.

## 2. The fundamental institution: RDA

**Regional Decentralized Authoritarianism** (Chenggang Xu) = **political centralization + economic decentralization**.

| Political centralization | Economic decentralization |
|---|---|
| Appointment of officials | Local development responsibility |
| Personnel control | Resource allocation |
| Strategic policies | Policy implementation |

### Why combine the two?

1. **Information problem:** local governments know local resources, firms and conditions better.
2. **Incentive problem:** the center needs a way to motivate local officials (the answer: promotion).

### Government structure

**Central → Province → Prefecture → County → Township.** Most economic management happens at the subnational levels. Chinese local governments are unusually involved in the economy: attracting investment, building infrastructure, developing industry, implementing reforms. They became the **engines of development**.

**Basic growth mechanism:** local autonomy → economic competition → growth.

## 3. Historical origins

**Imperial China → Mao period → post-1978 reform.**

### Imperial legacy

- Same core pattern: **central control of personnel + local administrative discretion**. Officials were appointed by the Emperor but ran local affairs freely.
- **Why delegate?** Slow communication and transport meant local officials had better information and needed discretion.

### Bai and Jia (2023): choosing provincial capitals

A provincial capital is both the **administrative center** of the province and the **relay** for resources and information between the center and the prefectures.

- Moving taxes costs money: collecting from each prefecture j to capital i costs (tax share of j) × distance, plus sending a share **λ** of the tax on to the national capital.
- So the center picks the capital that minimizes **hierarchical distance** = Σ A_j·D(i,j) + λ·Σ A_j·D(i, national capital), where A_j is a prefecture's tax capacity (e.g. population).
- λ is about **0.3** in the Song and Ming dynasties and about **0.2** in the Qing and modern China. Hierarchical distance **predicts actual capital choices well**.

**Takeaway:** the state's geography was designed around the **information and transport** problem of governing from the center.

### The Mao era

- **Early 1950s:** Soviet-style planned economy (central planning, state ownership, administrative allocation). Highly centralized.
- **The problem with central planning:** weak incentives, slow adjustment, no experimentation.
- **Hayek's critique:** knowledge is **dispersed** across people and firms, often **tacit**, and constantly changing. No planner can gather it all. In a market, **prices** aggregate and transmit that information.
- **Great Leap Forward (1958–1962): unintended decentralization.** Central ministries handed many enterprises to local governments.
- This created **self-contained regional economies**: local governments controlled factories, labor and investment, and those capabilities survived.

**Crucial point:** in 1978 China was still planned, but **local governments already controlled substantial resources**. Reform did not *create* local governments as economic actors; it **empowered existing ones**.

### Why gradual reform?

| Political uncertainty | Economic uncertainty |
|---|---|
| Resistance to reform, ideological conflict | Unknown consequences, no market experience |

**Solution:** *experiment first, reform later*. Old system + new experiments; successful ones go national; local governments are the main agents.

**Political control (cadre appointment) + economic autonomy (local development) = the RDA regime.**

### Why decentralization generated growth

1. Local governments had better information.
2. Local officials had incentives to promote growth.
3. Regions competed with each other.
4. Successful policies spread through experiments.

**Three mechanisms:** regional competition · policy experimentation · local economic entrepreneurship.

## 4. Mechanism 1: regional competition (the promotion tournament)

- Local officials are rewarded by **political promotion**, not profit.
- Performance is judged **relative to other regions**: **Promotion_i = f(Growth_i − Growth_others)**.
- Better growth → higher promotion probability → more development effort.

### Why GDP growth became the criterion

- After 1978, "**take economic development as the central task**". Leaders visiting Europe and the US around 1978 saw how far behind Chinese industry was.
- GDP growth is **observable, measurable, comparable** across regions. (Ideally you'd reward effort and ability, but those are hard to see.)

### Why the tournament works in China

1. **Strong personnel control:** higher levels appoint, promote and dismiss (the **nested cadre system**, all the way down to township).
2. Officials **care about promotion**.
3. Regions have **similar economic structures** (each has agriculture, manufacturing, services, infrastructure), so comparison is fair.
4. Local governments **control important resources**.

**Benefits:** local governments attracted investment, built infrastructure, supported firms and experimented. Evidence shows better economic performance is linked to promotion.

### A problem: what if officials get money unrelated to growth?

## 5. Chen and Kung (2016): land revenue and the political resource curse

### The 1998 land reform

- All non-arable land is **owned by the state**.
- **Only local governments** can convert farmland to industrial or commercial use.
- Private users get **70-year use rights**.
- Local governments **keep all land-sale proceeds** ("extra-budgetary" revenue). From 1998, county governments came to rely heavily on land income.

### The hypothesis

**Land revenue → less dependence on growth → weaker promotion incentives.** Like a resource curse, but political.

### The study

- **Regression:** Turnover = α1·LandRev + α2·GDPGrowth + α3·LandRev×GDPGrowth + controls + fixed effects.
- **Outcome:** annual political turnover of **county party secretaries**, 1999–2008, coded **promotion = 3**, lateral transfer or stay = 2, retirement = 1, termination (corruption, resignation, death) = 0.
- **Data:** 1,753 counties in 24 provinces, 17,521 county-years, 4,390 party secretaries.

### Endogeneity and instruments

Land revenue is **endogenous**: ambition drives both land sales and promotion (omitted variable); promotion is known a year early (reverse causality); someone with slim chances may sell more land.

**Instruments:**

- **IV1:** geographic land-supply constraint (share of land too steep to build on, slope > 15°) × **national interest rate** changes (rate cuts raise land prices more where supply is tight; following Mian and Sufi 2011, Chaney et al. 2012).
- **IV2:** land-supply constraint × **house prices in the provincial capital** (a demand shock from nearby big cities).

### Findings

- Land revenue **reduces the weight of GDP growth** in promotion decisions.
- **Signaling channel:** land money goes to visible, quantifiable **"image projects"** (city construction, land development), **timed** to be seen before evaluation. Short tenures (< 5 years) encourage myopia.
- **Corruption channel:** land revenue goes with higher **administrative spending** and a **bigger bureaucracy**.
- **Adverse selection:** the officials who benefit most are those **politically connected** to superiors (same birthplace, past prefecture job) and those **over 44** (unlikely to be promoted anyway). No extra effect from education.

## 6. Mechanism 2: policy experimentation

**"Crossing the river by feeling the stones":** experiment → evaluate → expand. Experiments **reduced the cost of mistakes**.

### Why let local governments experiment?

1. Better local information (**Hayek**: local knowledge → decentralized experimentation).
2. Incentives to solve problems and take risks (the promotion system).
3. Failures stay **local**.
4. Successes can **spread**.

### Example 1: Household Responsibility System

- Before: agricultural collectives, weak incentives.
- **1978, Xiaogang village:** farmers **secretly** divided land among households and signed a contract.
- Big productivity gains; the center **legalized and expanded** it. Adoption spread quickly across counties (Chen, Li and Zhu 2025); agricultural productivity jumped (Lin 1990).

### Example 2: Special Economic Zones

- Foreign investment was politically controversial, so China opened **limited zones** instead of the whole country. **Shenzhen** is the famous one.
- SEZs offered: lower corporate taxes and tax holidays, duty-free imported inputs, new business models (joint ventures), land-use rights, flexible labor.
- Successful practices were expanded to other cities.

### Are experiments informative? Wang and Yang (2021)

**Problem:** selected regions ≠ average regions.

1. **Experimental sites are positively selected**, and selection is shaped by **career incentives** of the prefecture party secretary.
2. Participating regions **put in more effort** on that reform.
3. **Roll-out to non-experimental sites is much less successful.**

**Example:** *local fiscal empowerment* ("province-managing-county"). Provinces bypass prefectures and administer counties directly. **1,100+ counties** selected, 2003–2013. Early experimental sites did better than later adopters, so **selection biases the estimated effect upward**.

**Also:** gains in experimental regions may come **at the expense of other regions** (stealing migrants or investment).

### Summary of experimentation

Reduced uncertainty · used local information · allowed gradual change · **but experiments may not generalize**.

## 7. Limits: when the incentive system backfires

### The multi-task problem

When officials are evaluated on **measurable targets**, they focus on those and **neglect other tasks**. Governments care about growth + environment + social welfare + public services, but only growth was rewarded.

- **Early reform:** broad consensus that development was the priority, so GDP targets mobilized local governments well.
- **After rapid growth:** environmental damage, inequality, inefficient investment, fiscal risks. The old system fit less well.

### Land finance (again)

| Before land finance | After land finance |
|---|---|
| Promotion relied on economic performance | Land sales gave big fiscal revenue |
| GDP growth was a clear signal of effort | Less dependence on growth performance |

**Land revenue → fiscal autonomy → weaker growth incentives.**

### He et al. (2020): pollution targets and strategic compliance

- Rapid industrialization → severe pollution (Jia and Ku 2018). Growth incentives favored growth over the environment.
- The center added **environmental targets** (e.g. the 10th Five-Year Plan, 2001–2005, required a **10% cut in national COD** water emissions), passed down from provinces to prefectures and counties and built into **promotion criteria**.
- **The catch:** water quality is measured at **monitoring stations**, which only capture pollution from **upstream**.
- So officials enforced rules hard on firms **just upstream** of stations and went easy on firms **just downstream**.
- **Method:** regression discontinuity around monitoring stations (distance negative = upstream, positive = downstream).
- **Results:** **lower TFP for upstream firms in polluting industries**, no effect in non-polluting industries; pollution fell where it was measured.

**Lesson:** political targets get *targeted enforcement*, not necessarily the most efficient environmental outcome.

## 8. Big picture

| China's growth was supported by | The same institutions created |
|---|---|
| Regional decentralization | **Land dependence** |
| Political centralization | **Environmental problems** |
| Competition | **Incentive conflicts** |
| Experimentation | |
