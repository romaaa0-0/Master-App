# Lecture 1: Introduction to Chinese Economy

> **In one sentence:** in four decades China went from one of the world's poorest economies to the second largest, and this course asks what explains that growth, which institutions made it possible, and whether it can last.

## 1. The big question

- **1978:** China was one of the poorest economies in the world.
- **2020s:** the **second-largest economy**, the **largest manufacturer and exporter** (from textiles to EVs and electronics), and fast progress in AI and self-driving.
- China now files more **critical technology patents** than the US (it overtook the US in the late 2010s, per Lerner et al. 2026).

**Key question of the course:** *what explains China's remarkable economic growth?*

## 2. Measuring prosperity: GDP

**GDP** = the market value of all **final** goods and services produced within a country in a given period (China: **$17.73 trillion in 2021**).

- **GDP per capita** is the usual measure of residents' well-being. Easy to compare, but it misses a lot: inequality, underemployment, pollution, loss of farmland, commuting costs, the value of parenting…

### Three equivalent ways to compute GDP (the wine economy)

Imagine the whole economy is one firm making wine.

| Approach | What you count |
|---|---|
| **Production** | Bottles of wine **produced** in the year |
| **Expenditure** | Bottles of wine **sold** to consumers |
| **Income** | **Income** of workers + firm owners (economic vs. accounting profit) |

All three give the same number.

### Comparing GDP across countries: PPP

- The same basket costs different amounts in different countries (think **Big Mac Index**).
- **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** uses price surveys from the **International Comparison Program (ICP)**: similar-quality goods, consumption and investment prices, tradable and non-tradable goods.
- Comparing prices between poor and rich countries is hard (**Deaton & Heston 2010**).

## 3. The great transformation

| Period | Phase | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **1949–1978** | Planned economy | State ownership, central planning |
| **1978–today** | Reform and opening | Market reforms, private sector |
| **2001–today** | Global integration | **WTO accession**, export boom |
| **Today** | Global power | Innovation, new challenges |

From the "**Great Divergence**" (China falling far behind the UK and US after 1800) to the "**Growth Miracle**" (GDP per capita shooting up after 1978). Shenzhen's Houhai went from fishing village (1980s) to skyline.

### The miracle in three numbers

| ≈ **10%** | **800+ million** | **#1** |
|---|---|---|
| Average GDP growth, 1978–2010 | People lifted out of extreme poverty | Manufacturing exporter in the world |

## 4. Growth accounting: what drove it?

**Framework (Hall and Jones, 1999):**

**Y = K^α × (A·H)^(1−α)**, which rearranges to output per worker:

**Y/L = A × (K/Y)^(α/(1−α)) × (H/L)**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Y | GDP |
| L | Employment |
| K | Capital |
| H | Quality-adjusted employment (human capital) |
| A | **TFP** (total factor productivity) |

So growth in output per worker comes from three sources:

| Capital | Quality-adjusted labor | Productivity (TFP) |
|---|---|---|
| Investment, infrastructure, foreign capital | Education, training, health | Technology, reallocation, efficiency gains |

**What the data show (relative to the US):** GDP per capita climbing steadily; human capital rising slowly; **TFP still low** and rising only gently; **capital-output ratio rising sharply**, especially after 2008.

### Capital: the investment-driven model

- **National income identity:** Y = C + I + G + NX (NX = exports − imports).
- **Capital accumulation:** K(t+1) = (1 − δ)K(t) + I(t). Today's investment becomes tomorrow's capital, minus depreciation δ.
- China's investment share of GDP is **unusually high** compared with other economies, fed by a **high household saving rate** and **massive infrastructure** spending.
- **Alwyn Young (1995):** the Asian miracle economies grew mainly by **accumulating factors** (capital, labor), not through technology. A warning: factor accumulation has diminishing returns.

### Human capital

- Free primary and middle school.
- **College expansion.**
- **One-child policy → quantity-quality tradeoff:** fewer children, more invested in each.
- Better health → higher productivity and more incentive to invest in education.

### Productivity: two sources

1. **Structural transformation:** labor moved from **low-productivity agriculture** to **higher-productivity manufacturing**. Just moving people raises aggregate productivity.
2. **Productivity catch-up:** adopting foreign technology, learning from global markets, innovation.

## 5. Opening to the global economy

**Before 1978:** a closed economy. Little trade, restricted foreign investment, SOEs dominating, central planning.

**Opening was gradual, not overnight:**

1. Experiment with market reforms (**Special Economic Zones**, 1980s)
2. Expand what works
3. Integrate globally (**WTO accession, 2001**) → deep integration

**Key idea:** gradual reform → learning and adaptation.

### How globalization drove growth

- **Export-oriented industrialization:** Exports → manufacturing expansion → growth. China's share of world exports jumps after 2001.
- **Global value chains:** design (US) → components (Japan/Korea) → **assembly (China)** → global consumers. China's edge: huge manufacturing capacity, dense supplier networks, access to world markets.
- **FDI** brought capital, technology, management practices, market access and trained workers: **foreign investment → technology transfer → higher productivity**.

**The export-led model:** FDI + globalization + manufacturing → rapid growth.

## 6. What makes China different?

### Not just another East Asian miracle

| | Japan | Korea | China |
|---|---|---|---|
| Growth strategy | Technology catch-up | Export-led | Export + investment + reform |
| Main firms | Large private firms | Chaebol | State + private firms |
| Role of government | Industrial coordination | Strategic support | Market + state coordination |

**Key difference:** China combined **market-oriented reforms** with a **strong state**. Neither pure planning nor pure laissez-faire.

### Five distinctive features

1. **State–market combination:** market competition and private entrepreneurship, plus state coordination and industrial policy.
2. **Local government competition:** local governments act like **entrepreneurs**, competing for investment, projects and growth (bottom-up policy → growth).
3. **Gradual reform by experimentation:** central planning → local experiments (SEZs, **TVEs**, township and village enterprises) → market economy. *Experiment first, expand later.*
4. **Dual economy:** an **old sector** (SOEs, planned allocation, low productivity) and a **new sector** (private, export-oriented, higher productivity). Growth of the new sector raises aggregate productivity.
5. **Manufacturing scale:** big labor force + big domestic market + dense clusters + global access → **economies of scale**, lower costs, competitiveness. The "factory of the world".

### The growth model in one line

**Growth = capital accumulation + structural transformation + globalization + productivity catch-up + state coordination**

- China's experience offers lessons for developing countries (Vietnam emulates parts of it), and **industrial policy** is back in fashion, even in the US.
